SwapSeq Atlas · version 0.4

Can public microbiome data be checked for swapped samples?

When the same people give samples again and again, each person's microbes work like a fingerprint, so a tube with the wrong label stands out. We tested how well that works on — public studies' tables, — samples in all. The answer depends mostly on which part of the body the samples come from.

How the test works

1

Take a public study

Each colour is one person. Every person gave several samples over weeks or months.

2

Secretly swap a few labels

We exchange the labels of a few real samples, about 1 in 100, as a lab mix-up would.

3

Count what SwapSeq finds

SwapSeq does not know which ones we swapped. We repeat this 10 times per table and count how many it catches.

What it found in the data as published

From flags to strong candidates

A flag is a sample that looks like a different person. It becomes a strong candidate when it also shares rare strains with that person, and the table is one SwapSeq can check well.

What this means

  • It finds a real swap.The diet study's authors fixed one tube swap before publishing. With the old labels put back, SwapSeq flags exactly that pair (— flags, nothing else).
  • Same stool, different labels.In the storage study every tube from one donor holds the same stool, yet two donors' tubes look exchanged, with rare strains to back it.
  • A flag alone is not proof.The transplant study has — flags and — with rare-strain support: treatment wiped out those patients' gut microbes, so they stop looking like anyone.

Behind the numbers

People must look like themselves

Each dot is one table. Re-identified means a sample's closest match is another sample from the same person. When people keep looking like themselves (85% or more), SwapSeq catches most hidden swaps. Below 75% their microbes change too much and it catches almost none. Tap a dot for the table.

Is SwapSeq's power figure right?

After each run SwapSeq tells you its power: the share of swaps it would catch in your table. Each dot is one table, placed by how far that figure was from the catch rate measured here. Dots on the green line are spot on. Version 1.0 often said near 0% for tables it checks well, so many of its dots sit far to the left. Version 1.1 measures power on hidden test errors (the November update also counts swap pairs), and the average gap fell from — to — points.

Method, limits and sources
Who made this
Mankirat Singh, with SwapSeq 1.1.1, second November update (reciprocal swap pairs become strong flags in tables that separate swaps well, also in small tables), for the flags, catch rates and power figure; the version 1.0 power figure is shown for comparison (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23963612, RRID:SCR_029248). Study-level results only: sample IDs stay private until each study's authors have been told.
Data
Public longitudinal tables hosted on GitHub with a person ID per sample: the five SwapSeq validation studies, the GPZ longitudinal collection, the MITRE example data, the parafac4microbiome data, MicrobeDS, the Borenstein lab curated collection, six longitudinal shotgun tables (MetaPhlAn profiles) released by Ye et al. (2026; their HMP2 IBD table is left out because the Atlas already holds HMP2), and, new in version 0.4, five body sites of the first Human Microbiome Project and a controlled-feeding study from the Knights lab MLRepo. Three tables (Hagan, Palleja, Taur) could not be fetched again for version 0.4 and keep their version 0.3 results; all three are tables SwapSeq cannot check. Samples under 1,000 reads were dropped. Tables given as relative abundance were scaled to counts.
Hidden test swaps
About 0.3% of samples in reciprocal swaps and 0.2% in one-way relabels per repeat; tables under 500 samples get a single error per repeat (a swap or a relabel, alternating) and 80 repeats, larger tables 20 repeats (10 above 1,500 samples). Errors are drawn from people with three or more samples; default settings. Version 0.2 planted at least three swaps and two relabels from 300 samples up, up to 2.7% errors at once in mid-size tables, which under-rated them. "Swaps caught" counts strong flags. "With check list" adds samples sent for a records review.
False flags
Strong flags on samples whose labels were not changed, per 1,000 samples, leaving out samples already flagged on the data as published.
Rare-strain support
The flagged sample shares at least three rare features (present in 5% or fewer of samples) with the person it looks like, and at least twice as many as with its own label.
Left out
Tables only available as R objects or on sites this build could not reach (Qiita, ENA, figshare), the HMP1 16S table (no person ID per sample) and one-sample-per-person cohorts. The diet study with its labels restored to before the authors' correction is a positive control, shown in the table but not counted in the totals.
Limits
A low grade means SwapSeq cannot vouch for a table either way, not that the data are poor. Reprocessed tables can score differently from the authors' own. Catch rates in small tables carry several points of noise. Flags are candidates for a records check, not proof of error.
