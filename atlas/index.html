Can public microbiome data be checked for swapped samples?

When the same people give samples again and again, each person's microbes work like a fingerprint, so a tube with the wrong label stands out. We tested how well that works on — public studies' tables, — samples in all. The answer depends mostly on which part of the body the samples come from.

How the test works

1

Take a public study

Each colour is one person. Every person gave several samples over weeks or months.

2

Secretly swap a few labels

We exchange the labels of a few real samples, about 1 in 100, as a lab mix-up would.

3

Count what SwapSeq finds

SwapSeq does not know which ones we swapped. We repeat this 10 times per table and count how many it catches.

What it found in the data as published

From flags to strong candidates

A flag is a sample that looks like a different person. It becomes a strong candidate when it also shares rare strains with that person, and the table is one SwapSeq can check well.

What this means

  • It finds a real swap.The diet study's authors fixed one tube swap before publishing. With the old labels put back, SwapSeq flags exactly that pair (— flags, nothing else).
  • Same stool, different labels.In the storage study every tube from one donor holds the same stool, yet two donors' tubes look exchanged, with rare strains to back it.
  • A flag alone is not proof.The transplant study has — flags and — with rare-strain support: treatment wiped out those patients' gut microbes, so they stop looking like anyone.

Method, limits and sources
Who made this
Mankirat Singh, with SwapSeq 1.0.1 for the flags and catch rates, and SwapSeq 1.1.1 for the measured power figure (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23963612, RRID:SCR_029248). Study-level results only: sample IDs stay private until each study's authors have been told.
Data
Public longitudinal tables hosted on GitHub with a person ID per sample: the five SwapSeq validation studies, the GPZ longitudinal collection, the MITRE example data, the parafac4microbiome data, MicrobeDS and the Borenstein lab curated collection. Samples under 1,000 reads were dropped. Tables given as relative abundance were scaled to counts.
Hidden test swaps
About 0.6% of samples in reciprocal swaps and 0.2% in one-way relabels per repeat (one of each in tables under 300 samples), drawn from people with three or more samples; default settings; 10 repeats per table. "Swaps caught" counts strong flags. "With check list" adds samples sent for a records review.
False flags
Strong flags on samples whose labels were not changed, per 1,000 samples, leaving out samples already flagged on the data as published.
Rare-strain support
The flagged sample shares at least three rare features (present in 5% or fewer of samples) with the person it looks like, and at least twice as many as with its own label.
Left out
Tables only available as R objects or on sites this build could not reach (Qiita, ENA, figshare), the HMP1 16S table (no person ID per sample) and one-sample-per-person cohorts. The diet study with its labels restored to before the authors' correction is a positive control, shown in the table but not counted in the totals.
Limits
A low grade means SwapSeq cannot vouch for a table either way, not that the data are poor. Reprocessed tables can score differently from the authors' own. Flags are candidates for a records check, not proof of error.
