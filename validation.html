How SwapSeq works, and how well

SwapSeq checks whether each sample in a longitudinal microbiome study came from the person on its label. This page sets out the method, the public data it was tested on, how errors were planted, and every result, including where it does poorly.

01

What it checks

In a study that samples the same people over time, each person's microbiome is a fingerprint. Their own samples resemble each other more than they resemble anyone else's. A sample that resembles somebody else better than its own person is a sign that something went wrong between the bench and the spreadsheet.

You give SwapSeq a feature table (ASVs, OTUs or taxa, as counts) and the sample sheet with a column saying who each sample came from. A plate and well column, a household column and a visit column are optional. Every person needs at least two samples.

Swapped tubes

Two samples carry each other's labels. SwapSeq names both and the pair.

Wrong labels

One sample carries someone else's label. SwapSeq says who it looks like.

Sample-sheet shifts

A run of tubes each carries its neighbour's label. SwapSeq reports the run and its wells.

02

Method

Everything runs in the browser, in plain JavaScript with no server. The same code (method/core.mjs) runs the benchmarks below.

  1. Two fingerprints of every sample

    Soft Jaccard keeps the presence of low-abundance strains, the part of a microbiome most specific to a person, and removes the effect of unequal sequencing depth without random rarefaction. Each feature gets its expected presence after subsampling to r reads:

    ek = 1 − (1 − r / N)ck    r = median depth ÷ 8
    dJ(a,b) = 1 − Σ min(ea, eb) ⁄ Σ max(ea, eb)

    N is the sample's depth and ck its count for feature k. The second fingerprint is the Aitchison distance: Euclidean distance after a centred log-ratio transform of counts + 0.5, on features present in at least 5% of samples (up to the 3,000 most common).

  2. Does a sample fit its own person?

    si = log( down ⁄ dother )

    down is the soft Jaccard distance from sample i to the nearest other sample with the same label, and dother the distance to the nearest sample of anyone else. A score above zero means the sample sits closer to someone else; that person is reported as who it looks like.

  3. Would swapping two labels fix both?

    wij = log( owni ⁄ d(i, person of j) ) + log( ownj ⁄ d(j, person of i) )

    Averaged over both fingerprints, with the two samples themselves left out of each person's set. The maximum over every j with a different label is the swap score, and that j is the likely partner. A swap is reported only when two samples name each other and both carry swap evidence.

  4. Learn what an error looks like in this study

    The same score means different things on tongue swabs and on stool, or with daily and monthly sampling. So SwapSeq plants errors in a copy of the user's own labels, between 300 and 1,500 one-way relabels and as many reciprocal swaps, and records their scores. With f1 the density of planted-error scores and g the density of all observed scores:

    P(error | s) = min( 1, π · f1(s) ⁄ g(s) )    π = 1% expected error rate

    Densities are Gaussian kernel estimates. P is made non-decreasing in the score, but only where observed scores actually lie, so a gap in the tail cannot create a false spike. Version 1.0 reported the share of planted scores that would reach a strong flag as the study's power. Version 1.1 instead measures power by hiding test errors in copies of the labels and rerunning the check (see Validation).

    Strong flag

    Kernel probability ≥ 0.5. Built for near-zero false alarms.

    Check list

    A logistic fit of the same ratio ≥ 0.5, or kernel probability ≥ 0.2. Catches more, with some natural look-alikes. Hidden when fewer than 8 people have two or more samples.

  5. Evidence for each flag

    • Rare features. Of a flagged sample's rare features (in ≤ 5% of samples, with at least 2 reads), how many are also found in its own person and how many in the person it looks like.
    • Households. If the look-alike lives in the same household, a strong flag moves to the check list.
    • Wells. For a swap pair on one plate: adjacent, mirrored across the plate, diagonal or apart.
    • Sheet shifts. Tubes are ordered down plate columns, along rows, or by a plating-order number. A run of three or more consecutive tubes, each closer to someone else and that someone being the person on its neighbour's label, is reported as a shift, and its tubes join the check list.

04

Test design

  • Errors at about a 1% rate. In each repeat, 3 reciprocal swaps and 2 one-way relabels were planted in a copy of the labels; tables under 300 samples got 1 of each, and before/after designs 1 swap.
  • 30 repeats per table with different random seeds (60 for the smaller IMP table).
  • Clean ground truth. Errors were planted only into people with at least three samples, and never into samples SwapSeq already flagged on the untouched data.
  • What was counted. The share of planted samples that reached a strong flag, and a flag or the check list. The share of swap pairs named exactly. False alarms per 1,000 untouched samples.
  • Same settings throughout. The defaults the tool ships with: 1% expected error rate, flag at probability 0.5.
  • A baseline. The identical pipeline with Bray-Curtis distance in place of the two fingerprints, the distance used by a recent mislabel detector for shotgun data. This isolates what the fingerprints add.
SwapSeq's settings were chosen while working on these tables, so the numbers below are development results, not a held-out test. New studies are the real test, which is why feedback is wanted.
05

Results

On 16S data from adults, SwapSeq strongly flagged 40–92% of swapped samples, against 0–38% for Bray-Curtis in the same pipeline. On daily shotgun data, where every distance works well, the gap is small: 88–94% against 84–92%. Single mislabels are harder because there is no partner to confirm them; the check list recovers many of them.

Power is now measured on your own table (version 1.1)

Every run reports how many swaps SwapSeq could catch in that study. In version 1.0 this figure was read off the calibration. It matched the measured catch rate when a table already held errors (the 13 benchmark tables below, within 7 points on average), but on clean tables it could read close to 0% for a table SwapSeq checks well. The SwapSeq Atlas found this across 30 public tables in November 2026.

Version 1.1 measures power directly: it hides one swap or one relabel at a time in copies of the user's labels, reruns the full check on the same distances, and counts what is caught. Against a separate measurement on the same 30 tables, the average gap on clean labels fell from 31 to 9 points, and no table SwapSeq checks well is shown below 20%. When a table already holds 1-2% errors, near-certain flags are set aside before the tests are hidden (1.1.1), so the tests do not compete with errors SwapSeq has already found; the average gap there is 9 points, against 7 for 1.0, about the noise between two repeat measurements (8 points). It adds a few seconds to a typical run of a few hundred samples, and one to two minutes for 2,000 or more.

Where it is weaker

  • Tongue and palm. Single mislabels: 13% and 10% strongly flagged, 60% and 47% with the check list.
  • IMP mislabels. 12% strongly flagged, 48% with the check list, where Bray-Curtis flagged 23%. One of the few places the baseline does better.
  • Before/after with two samples each. With only two samples, a swap makes both people inconsistent and SwapSeq can name the two people more reliably than the tube. In the diet study's two-per-person design nothing reached a strong flag; 42% reached the check list.
  • Check list in low-signal data. In infants and mice the check list carries 13–16 false entries per 1,000 samples. Measured power is near zero there, and the tool says so.
06

Plate errors

Lab errors follow the plate. Neighbouring tubes get swapped, and a sample sheet that slips by one row shifts every label after it. These patterns were planted on the real plating order of the diet study (526 tubes over 6 plates), 30 repeats each.

Runs of five or more tubes were named as a shift in 90–100% of repeats. Three-tube runs were still flagged tube by tube (82%) but not named as a shift. No repeat produced a false shift report.

08

On the untouched data

Run on the published tables as they are, SwapSeq gave 12 strong flags among 4,732 samples. Ten were in the SMP tongue table, one in gut and one in palm; none in the IMP, diet, infant or mouse tables.

These may be real mix-ups or natural look-alikes. Tongue swabs carry the weakest individual signal of the four SMP sites, so its flags deserve the most caution. They have not been checked against lab records, so they are reported here as counts only. The SMP authors list samples they removed for suspected mix-ups; those samples are not in any published table, so they could not be used as a check.

09

Tried and dropped

Each idea below was built and run on the same benchmark. None caught more errors at the same false-alarm rate, or it broke a study design, so none shipped.

  • Weighting rare features (IDF)Rare features already drive soft Jaccard; extra weight added noise.
  • Whole-person count modelA negative binomial model of each person's counts. Slower and no better.
  • Isotonic and tail-area calibrationLess smooth than the kernel, with worse false-alarm control at a 1% prior.
  • Estimating the error rate (EM)Unstable when errors are rare, which is exactly the normal case.
  • Ignoring same-visit look-alikesHelps when one batch makes samples look alike, but breaks before/after designs with two samples each.
  • Blending nearest and median distanceDiluted the nearest-neighbour signal that a swap leaves.
  • An ensemble of six scoresStacking nearest-neighbour, rare-taxon, Bray-Curtis, Aitchison, fingerprint and time scores caught 5–7 more points of swaps, but overfitted small tables (16 false flags per 1,000 at 103 samples) and lost the calibrated probability.
  • Time-aware fingerprintsRemoving what all samples of a similar age share, tested on 28 Atlas tables. On the 14 tables where SwapSeq caught under 20%, strong flags rose only from 2.6% to 4.2%, while false check-list entries rose from 0.3 to 4.1 per 1,000. Checkable tables were unchanged.
  • Letting the data raise the error rateEstimating the error rate from the upper tail and using it when above 1% (capped at 3%). With 3% of samples swapped it caught more, but on the published labels it added strong flags in 5 of 29 tables (up to 9 in one) and up to 37 other samples per 1,000 in one table. Choosing a higher expected error rate yourself, when the tool says the table holds many swap pairs, does the same job openly.
  • A check for mixtures of two peopleA sample holding material from two people (cross-contamination, pooled tubes). Tested on 23 public tables with mixtures made in the computer. A statistic built on another person’s personal taxa caught a median of 26% of half-and-half mixtures and 8% at one part in ten; half or more in only 8 of 23 tables. An ordinary sample also picks up taxa that someone else carries, so the two look alike. The tool as it is lists 37% of half-and-half mixtures, 18% at 30% and 3% at 10%, and names the right second person when it does. Numbers are in validation/mixtures.

Shotgun data: strain markers and host DNA

Where people stop looking like themselves (newborns, heavy antibiotics, lab animals from one donor), no method based on a species or ASV table caught more than a few per cent of swaps. Shotgun data carry two more kinds of identity, and SwapSeq can now use both.

Strain markers. A presence table of strain markers, genes or SNVs (for example MetaPhlAn marker presence) is compared with the Jaccard distance, and with a second Jaccard distance in which each marker is weighted by −log(prevalence), so that a shared rare marker counts for more. Only markers present in at least 2 samples and at most half of them are used. Scoring and calibration are unchanged.

That test is done, and the answer is that strain markers help only a little. In adults and older children they matched the species table. In four infant and newborn cohorts they raised strong flags in two and left two at zero. Infant gut communities change faster than a marker fingerprint can follow, so strain markers do not make SwapSeq a check for newborn or infant studies.

Host DNA (beta). Shotgun reads carry some of the person's own DNA, usually one read at a few thousand common SNPs. Two single samples then rarely share sites, so, as in single-cell demultiplexing, each sample's reads are scored against every person's pooled reads from all their other samples, with Hardy–Weinberg genotype priors and a 1% read-error rate. Each person's match is placed between two levels read off the data: a stranger and the same person, with a first-degree relative in between. A sample is flagged only when its own label is clearly not the same person, another person clearly is, and that person explains its reads at least e8 (about 3,000) times better. Samples that clearly do not fit their label are left out of the pools in a second pass, so a swapped-in sample does not blur its new person’s genotypes. When the label and another person both fit, SwapSeq reports “identical DNA” (identical twins, or one person under two IDs) instead of flagging.

Host reads are human genetic data: use them only within your ethics approval; SwapSeq processes them on your own computer.

Several errors at once, and duplicates

Several errors in one table. The calibration assumes about 1% errors. When a table holds more, a strong flag needs more evidence than the table can give, and strong flags alone caught few: with 3% of samples swapped in the seven longitudinal shotgun tables of Ye et al. (2026), 5%. A swap leaves two samples that each fit the other's person, and when both sit on the check list and point at each other that pattern is far rarer by chance than either sample alone. Such pairs are now strong flags, but only in tables where planted swaps stand out well: at least 70% of planted swap scores above the 95th percentile of the table's own scores. Most samples are correct even at a few per cent errors, so this gate stays stable when the table holds several errors (the measured power does not). When more than 1.5 times the expected rate of samples sit in swap pairs, the tool says so and suggests a higher expected error rate.

For comparison, Find-abnormality (Ye et al. 2026) flagged 96% of hidden swaps in the seven shotgun tables, and 52 other samples per 1,000; SwapSeq set to a 3% expected rate flagged 91% with 18 per 1,000.

The same test at matched settings. Find-abnormality has one setting and casts a wide net. Run with a net as wide, the current SwapSeq lists more of the hidden swaps and fewer other samples: at the 5% setting, strong flags plus the check list caught 99% with 30 other samples per 1,000 (strong flags alone 94% with 21), and at 3% they caught 97% with 24, against 96% with 52. At its default 1% SwapSeq caught fewer (75% as strong flags, 89% with the check list) with 9 and 17 other samples per 1,000. Read this with care: the SwapSeq setting was chosen after seeing the results, which favours SwapSeq; in one of the seven tables Find-abnormality still found more (95% against 90%) and in another it flagged fewer other samples; and a wider net costs work everywhere, since on the published labels of 23 other public tables the 5% setting lists 31 samples per 1,000 against 7.5 at 1%. Numbers and script are in validation/ye2026_comparison.

Duplicates. Two samples labelled as different people whose communities are near-identical are listed as possible duplicates: one tube sequenced twice under two names, a reused extract or a file uploaded twice. The two must be each other's nearest neighbour among other people's samples and, on both distances, closer than almost any pair from different people (log distance below the median minus 5 MAD); with repeat samples, also closer than 0.7 times the distance from either sample to its own person's nearest other sample. In the 15 shotgun tables of Ye et al., a sample re-sequenced under another person's name (every abundance jittered, rare taxa dropped at random) was found in 102 of 105 tests in cross-sectional tables and 66 of 105 in longitudinal ones, where a person's consecutive samples can be just as close. No pair was reported that was not planted. In tables with repeat samples, 7 of 105 planted mislabels also showed up as a near-identical pair, so the message names both causes. On the published labels of the cross-sectional tables, 8 of the 9 pairs SwapSeq lists are also on Find-abnormality's duplicate list, which has 10.

Small studies, broken files and a code review

Small studies. One swap in a 30-sample table is already 7% errors, far above the expected 1%, so the calibrated probability could not reach a strong flag however clear the swap was. In tables that separate planted swaps very well (at least 90% of planted swap scores above a cut that ignores the top max(5%, 4) observed scores, and at least 75% of samples closer to their own person than to anyone else), two samples that are each other's best swap partner and both score like a typical planted swap are now reported as a swap pair. Both people need three or more samples: with two per person, the data cannot say which pair was swapped.

Broken and unusual files. A battery of 60 input cases runs with every change (cli/test_inputs.mjs): Windows, Unix and old-Mac line ends, a byte-order mark, quoted fields, semicolon-separated files, a trailing tab, R tables without a corner cell, a taxonomy column, decimal and scientific notation, relative abundances, empty cells, missing subject labels, people with one sample, BIOM 1.0, and files that must be refused with a clear message (duplicate sample IDs, text or negative numbers in a count cell, a line with too few columns, an empty file, an Excel or BIOM 2 file, a subject column where nobody has two samples, an output name that would overwrite the input). Before this update, 17 of the 60 failed: two crashed, and the others refused a readable file, missed the swap, or accepted a bad file without warning. The worst: a header one cell short shifted every sample ID by one column, and the output could overwrite the input table.

Independent code review. A reviewer who had not seen the code being written was asked to find defects and prove each one with a script. It reported 18; 17 are fixed, among them missing subject labels in R being treated as one person, two all-zero samples reported as duplicates, a duplicate check that fired in tables of two or three samples, household look-alikes promoted as swap pairs, and a host-DNA flag written as “not checked”. One is left as it is: results can differ in the third decimal when the same samples are given in another column order, because the planted errors are drawn in file order.

Duplicates in 16S and other count tables. In 28 count tables, a sample re-sequenced under another person's name (new depth, every count jittered and drawn again) was found in 309 of 420 tests. No pair was reported that was not planted, and 5 of 420 planted mislabels showed up as a duplicate. It works poorly where different people's samples are nearly alike anyway or where a person's own samples are just as close: vaginal swabs (1 of 15), skin behind the ear (0 of 15) and same-stool aliquots (0 of 15).

More public data. Atlas version 0.4 adds five body sites of the first Human Microbiome Project and a controlled-feeding study (42 tables, 20,974 samples). HMP1 stool caught 99% of hidden swaps, dental plaque 92%, tongue and nostril 88%, skin behind the ear 75%. A twin study with two samples per person (Turnbaugh 2009, 270 samples) cannot be graded by the Atlas test; with family given as household, it gave 3 strong flags and one possible duplicate on the published labels.

Limits

  • One confirmed real error. Everything else is planted. Planted errors are clean label exchanges; real mix-ups can also be partial, such as cross-contamination or a split sample.
  • Narrow evidence base. Adult 16S results come from two studies, shotgun from one, and plate patterns from one real plating order.
  • Development results. Settings were chosen on these tables.
  • Needs repeat samples. At least two per person, better three. People who live together, antibiotics and illness all blur the fingerprint.
  • Memory. All pairwise distances are held in the browser, so tables of many thousands of samples need a lot of memory. The largest table in this benchmark had 875 samples; the SwapSeq Atlas ran tables of up to 2,479 samples, and the browser accepts up to 4,000 per check.
  • Host DNA is new. The host-DNA check is tested on simulated data only.
  • Mixtures. A sample holding material from two people is mostly missed: about a third of half-and-half mixtures are listed, and almost none at one part in ten (section 09).
  • Newborns and infants. Species tables catch few or no swaps there, and strain markers helped only a little (section 10). Do not rely on SwapSeq for these studies.
  • Many errors at once. At the default 1% expected rate, strong flags catch fewer swaps when several per cent of a table are wrong; follow the tool's note and raise the expected rate. Duplicates are found less often in longitudinal tables than in cross-sectional ones.
  • Small studies. Below about 25 samples, or with two samples per person, a single swap is often not a strong flag even in sample types SwapSeq checks well; it usually sits on the check list.
  • A flag is a reason to check. Compare it with plate maps, extraction batches and sample sheets before relabelling or dropping anything. SwapSeq is a research QC tool, not a medical device.

Reproduce

Node.js 22 and nothing else. Clone the five source repositories into one folder (the layout is in method/load.mjs), then:

git clone https://github.com/bymankirat/swapseq
cd swapseq
export SWAPSEQ_DATA=~/swapseq-data

# planted-error benchmark: 30 repeats, 1% prior
SETS=gut,tongue,palm,forehead,ecam,gut2,forehead2 node method/bench.mjs 30 0.01
SETS=diet,dietsp,mdh,mdu,diet2 node method/bench.mjs 30 0.01
SETS=imp node method/bench.mjs 60 0.01

# same pipeline with Bray-Curtis
SETS=gut,tongue,palm,forehead,ecam,gut2,forehead2 node method/bench.mjs 30 0.01 bc

for L in 3 5 8; do node method/platebench.mjs burst 30 $L; done   # plate swaps and sheet shifts
node method/realswap.mjs       # the real diet-study swap

Every number on this page is in the validation folder as JSON.
