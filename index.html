Is every sample from the person on its label?

Tubes get swapped, labels get typed wrong, and sample sheets slip by a row. In a study where each person is sampled more than once, one mix-up quietly breaks every paired test. SwapSeq reads each person's microbial fingerprint and points to the samples that belong to someone else.

Results

How it works

Three checks, all run in your browser on your own table, and two more for shotgun data.

A fingerprint of rare taxa

Everyone carries a few uncommon ASVs that stay with them week after week. SwapSeq compares which ASVs each sample holds, at a matched sequencing depth, and their log-ratio profile. A sample closer to somebody else than to its own person stands out.

A test for swapped pairs

Two swapped tubes leave a signature: tube A fits person B and tube B fits person A. SwapSeq tests every pair and names the partner when swapping their labels back makes both fit again.

Calibrated on your own table

Before judging, SwapSeq swaps hundreds of labels in a copy of your data to learn what an error looks like there. It then gives strong flags with almost no false alarms, a list to check against lab records, and a measured rate of how many swaps it catches in your study, from test errors hidden in copies of your labels.

Shotgun data

Strain markers

Species are shared by many people, but strains are often personal. Choose Strain markers or genes as the data type and give a presence table, for example MetaPhlAn marker presence. SwapSeq then compares samples on the rare markers they share. In tests on public cohorts this matched the species table in adults and helped only a little in infants, so it does not make infant studies checkable.

Shotgun data · beta

Host DNA

Shotgun reads carry some of the person's own DNA, which does not change when their microbes do. SwapSeq matches each sample's reads at common human SNPs to every person's other samples. In a stress test on simulated people (uneven DNA, relatives, twins, contamination, mixed populations) it made almost no false flags and caught 85–100% of swaps with 2,000–4,000 covered SNP sites per sample and 6 samples per person, but few with only 2–3 samples per person. Identical twins cannot be told apart by DNA. It is not yet tested on real samples, and it is human genetic data: use it within your ethics approval. How to make the input.

Several errors

Swap pairs and a note

A swap leaves two samples that each fit the other's person. When both point at each other, SwapSeq now makes them strong flags in tables where test swaps stand out well. With 3% of samples swapped in seven shotgun tables, strong flags caught 41% of swaps instead of 5%. If a table holds more swap pairs than the expected error rate explains, SwapSeq says so; raising the rate to 2–5% then caught about 80%.

All study designs

Duplicates

Two samples labelled as different people with near-identical microbes are listed as possible duplicates: one tube sequenced twice, a reused extract or a file uploaded twice. It works with one sample per person too. With test duplicates planted in 15 public shotgun tables it found 97% in cross-sectional and 63% in longitudinal tables, and reported no pair that was not planted.

Tested on public studies

Known errors were planted in real published tables at about a 1% rate, 30 to 60 times each, and every result was counted. Bray-Curtis is the same pipeline with the distance used by a recent shotgun mislabel detector. Read the full methods and validation.

Before you trust a flag

  • Two or more samples per person. Three or more works better. With exactly two, SwapSeq can say which two people do not fit, but not always which tube moved.
  • People who live together share microbes. Add a household column and look-alikes from one home move to the check list.
  • Infants and same-donor animals carry little individual signal. SwapSeq says so with a low measured power and rarely flags anything.
  • Mixtures are mostly missed. A sample holding material from two people usually looks normal. In tests, about a third of half-and-half mixtures were listed and almost none at one part in ten.
  • Antibiotics and illness can make a sample unlike its owner without making it like anyone else. Those usually stay unflagged.
  • A flag is a reason to check the lab records. Look at plate maps, extraction batches and sample sheets before you relabel or drop anything.
  • Your data stays on your computer. Opening a file here reads it inside your browser. No server ever receives it.

Infants, antibiotics or lab animals? From taxa alone, no method we tried could check these: we also tested time-aware fingerprints that remove what all samples of the same age share, and they raised the catch on these tables only from 3% to 4%. With shotgun data, the host-DNA check (beta) is the way forward, because a person's own DNA does not change.

Use it in R or in a pipeline

The swapseqr package runs the same engine on a phyloseq object or a count matrix, so the check fits into an existing analysis script. Tested on Mac, Windows and Linux at every change.

On a server or in a pipeline, the command-line version needs only Node.js 18. It writes one row per sample, a summary and a report for your records, and --fail-on-flags stops the pipeline when a sample is strongly flagged. Nextflow and Snakemake examples.

# install once remotes::install_github("bymankirat/swapseqr") # check a phyloseq object res <- swapseqr::check_swaps(ps, subject = "subject_id") res[res$level != "ok", ] attr(res, "power") # measured on your own table # shotgun data: strain markers, host DNA (beta) swapseqr::check_swaps(ps_mk, "subject_id", type = "markers") swapseqr::check_swaps(ps, "subject_id", host = host_counts)
# command line (Node.js 18+) npx github:bymankirat/swapseq table.tsv metadata.tsv \ --subject subject_id --out swapseq --fail-on-flags
What changed in each version
  • 1.1.1, third November update · A report for your records: one HTML file with the flags, four figures, plain-language notes and space to write what the lab records showed; open it and print to PDF (the command line writes it too). Clearer plots on this page: an at-a-glance bar, the share of hidden errors found, a card for each listed sample showing whose rare microbes it carries, and a sample map that names listed samples. Flags and probabilities are unchanged. Tested and reported, not added: strain markers help only a little in infants, and a check for mixtures of two people was not reliable.
  • 1.1.1, second November update · Small studies: a clear swap in a table of 24–40 samples is now a strong flag (about 60–70% caught on real data, up from 1–30%). Sturdier file reading: semicolon and old-Mac files, R tables without a corner cell, and clear messages for duplicate sample IDs, text in a count cell, empty files and Excel files. Relative abundances are handled as such. 17 defects found by an independent code review were fixed.
  • 1.1.1, November update · Strain-marker mode and a host-DNA check (beta) for shotgun data; a command-line tool for pipelines; a duplicate check; swap pairs that point at each other are strong flags in tables that separate swaps well, which helps most when a table holds several errors.
  • 1.1.1 · Measured power no longer reads low when a table already holds errors.
  • 1.1.0 · Power is measured on your own table by hiding test errors in copies of your labels. Version 1.0 could show near 0% for tables it checks well.
  • 1.0.1 · Methods and validation page, feedback forms, citation details.
  • 1.0.0 · First release.

Privacy. SwapSeq has no server. Files you open are read by your browser and never sent anywhere. This page sets no cookies and uses no analytics or tracking. The website host may keep standard access logs, such as IP addresses, for security.

Use. SwapSeq is a quality-control tool for research data. It is not a medical device and must not be used for diagnosis or clinical decisions. Results come without warranty; check flags against your lab records. Free software under GPL-3.0-or-later.
